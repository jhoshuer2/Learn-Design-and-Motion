# Create a high-contrast event poster

**Assignment objective:** Apply contrast to build a clear reading hierarchy that stays legible at small size, and explain your decisions.

**Scenario:** A local creative conference, Open Studio 2026, needs a poster that communicates the event name, the date (Saturday 14 March), the location (Harbour Hall, Pier 4) and one call to action (register at openstudio.example). The poster will be printed A3 and also shared as a small image in group chats.

## Requirements
- Establish a clear reading hierarchy: event name, then date and place, then the call to action.
- Use typography intentionally, with no more than two typefaces.
- Apply contrast to separate the essential information from everything else.
- Keep essential text readable at the small preview size.
- Use spacing and alignment consistently.
- Explain three key design decisions in your rationale, each tied to contrast.

## Constraints
- Original work only. Any photograph or illustration must be one you are licensed to use.
- Do not copy a template or one of the lesson demos.
- Include all four pieces of event information. Do not invent extra details.

## Deliverables
- Final poster (required): PNG, JPEG or WebP export of the full poster.
- Small-scale preview (required): The same poster exported at about 400 px on its long side, as it would look in a chat thread.
- Process image: A sketch or an earlier version, if you want to show your process.

## Rubric: High-contrast event poster (pass mark 80%)

### information-hierarchy: Information hierarchy (weight 25%)

Does contrast make the reading order obvious: event name first, then date and place, then the call to action?
Strong (85-100): a viewer who glances for two seconds can say what the event is, when and where, and what to do. Each level of information differs clearly from the next in size, value or weight.
Adequate (60-84): the main message leads, but one or two levels compete or the order of date, place and action is unclear.
Weak (0-59): several elements have similar emphasis, or the most important information is not the most prominent.

### contrast: Contrast (value and colour) (weight 20%, REQUIRED minimum 60%)

Is there strong, deliberate value contrast between text and its background, and between figure and ground? Essential text should meet WCAG AA (4.5:1 normal, 3:1 large). Judge only what the submitted images show and say when you cannot measure.
Strong (85-100): all essential text clearly passes; contrast is used selectively, not everywhere.
Adequate (60-84): essential text is readable but one pair is borderline or contrast is applied unevenly.
Weak (0-59): essential text is hard to read, or contrast is so uniform that nothing separates.

### legibility-at-small-size: Legibility at small size (weight 15%, REQUIRED minimum 60%)

Judge the small-scale preview. Can the event name, date and place still be read, and does the hierarchy survive?
Strong (85-100): all essential information and the hierarchy survive in the preview.
Adequate (60-84): the hierarchy survives but one essential item is strained.
Weak (0-59): essential text becomes unreadable, or the preview is missing or not a smaller version of the final.

### typography: Typography (weight 15%)

Intentional choice and use of type: size, weight and spacing build hierarchy; line length and line height support reading; faces do not conflict.
Strong (85-100): a clear type system with purposeful contrasts.
Adequate (60-84): sound choices with minor inconsistencies.
Weak (0-59): arbitrary or conflicting type choices that harm reading.

### composition-and-spacing: Composition and spacing (weight 15%)

Alignment, grouping through proximity and space, margins, and balance. Related information is grouped, unrelated information is separated.
Strong (85-100): consistent alignment and spacing, clear groups, intentional use of empty space.
Adequate (60-84): mostly orderly, with some uneven spacing or loose alignment.
Weak (0-59): crowded or scattered layout with no clear grouping.

### design-rationale: Design rationale (weight 10%)

Does the written rationale explain three real design decisions and tie them to contrast and the audience? Judge reasoning, not writing style.
Strong (85-100): three specific decisions, each with a reason that matches what is visible in the work.
Adequate (60-84): decisions are named but reasons are thin, or one does not match the work.
Weak (0-59): generic statements, fewer than three decisions, or claims the work does not show.
