## {objective} What you will be able to do
By the end of this lesson you can:

- give each colour in a palette a role and a rough share of use;
- choose two type roles with clear hierarchy and a pairing that fits the brand;
- keep text readable against every background in the system;
- apply one system to formats of different shapes and sizes without it drifting.

## {concept} A system is a small set of choices with jobs
Brands look consistent because they use **few** colours and typefaces, each with a **role**, and use them the same way everywhere.

A practical palette has:

- a **primary** colour that carries recognition (used often, often as large areas or the logo);
- one or two **supporting** colours;
- **neutrals** (text colour, backgrounds, lines) that do most of the work quietly;
- an **accent** used sparingly for the thing you most want noticed.

A common teaching split is roughly 60% neutral or primary, 30% supporting and 10% accent. It is a starting point, not a rule: the point is that the proportions are **unequal and intended**.

For type, define two roles, usually a **headline** face and a **body** face, with sizes and weights for each. Clear difference between roles gives hierarchy. A pairing works when the two faces share a mood but differ in structure, or when one family offers both roles.

Two rules to carry forward:

1. Every colour pairing used for text needs enough **contrast** (about 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text). Check it, do not guess.
2. A system is only a system if **people can follow it without you**. Write the roles down.

## {realWorld} Where you meet this every day
Pick up a few items from the same local shop: a receipt, a bag, a leaflet, the shop front. If the shop is consistent, you can recognise all four from across the room, though they are different shapes and materials. Usually it comes down to the same primary colour, the same one or two typefaces and the same placement habits.

Now think of a shop where every sign is a different colour and typeface. Each sign might be fine alone, but nothing adds up to a brand.

## {guided} Try it with support
Use the fictional bakery from the brief in the module assessment (warm, honest, unfussy; families and office workers), or a business of your own. Work through these steps:

1. Choose a primary, one supporting colour, two neutrals and one accent. Write the hex value and the role for each.
2. Check the contrast of your text colour on each background it will meet. Write the ratios.
3. Choose a headline face and a body face. Write the sizes you would use for each.
4. Write one rule for each of: where the accent may be used, and which backgrounds the logo may sit on.

Keep your notes. They are the raw material for your brand sheet.

Choose a small brand you can see in the real world (not one used as an example here). Photograph or screenshot three different applications of it. Write a short analysis: what the palette and type roles seem to be; where the applications are consistent and where they drift; and one rule you would add to keep them aligned.
