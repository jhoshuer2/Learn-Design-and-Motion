## {objective} What you will be able to do
By the end of this lesson you can:

- pick body and heading sizes that read comfortably on a phone;
- measure the contrast of a text and background pair and compare it with the minimum;
- fix a failing pair by changing value, not only hue;
- check that no meaning depends on colour alone.

## {concept} Readable means measured, not guessed
People read on small screens in bad light, with tired eyes, and some with low vision or colour-vision differences. Readability therefore needs decisions you can **check**.

- **Size.** Body text of about **16 points or more** is a common minimum on a phone; captions can be smaller but not tiny. Leave comfortable line spacing.
- **Contrast ratio.** The accessibility guidelines (WCAG) set minimums for text against its background: about **4.5:1** for normal text and **3:1** for large text (roughly 18 points or 14 points bold) and for meaningful graphics such as icons that carry information.
- **Not colour alone.** If red means error and green means success, add a label or icon, so the meaning survives for someone who cannot tell them apart.
- **Hierarchy still applies.** Accessible does not mean flat: use size, weight and value to rank information while keeping every level readable.

You cannot judge a ratio by eye. Use a contrast checker, write the numbers down, and design to **pass with some margin**, because screens and lighting vary.

## {realWorld} Where you meet this every day
Placeholder text inside a form field, in a pale grey, is often the first thing to fail. So is white text on a bright yellow button, and light grey captions on photographs.

Think of a bank app that marks an overdue payment only in red. Someone with red-green colour blindness sees nothing different. A clear label such as "Overdue" with an icon fixes it for everyone.

## {guided} Try it with support
Take any screen you can open. Work through these steps:

1. Pick three text and background pairs (body, a caption and a button label).
2. Measure each with a contrast checker and write the ratios.
3. Mark each as pass or fail against 4.5:1 (or 3:1 for large text).
4. For one failing pair, change the value until it passes and note the new colours and ratio.

Keep your notes. You will use them in the independent task.

## {reflection} Reflect
Which of your measured pairs surprised you most: one that looked fine and failed, or one that looked weak and passed? What does that say about judging by eye?

## {feedback} What to practise next
If ratios felt abstract, measure ten pairs in apps you use and note how many fail. If colour-alone problems were hard to spot, switch a screenshot to greyscale and see what you lose. Next: the module assessment, one mobile screen that puts the whole module together.
