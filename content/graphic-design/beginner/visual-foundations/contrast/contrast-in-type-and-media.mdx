## {concept} Readability is contrast plus size plus time
Text is read, not just seen, so contrast has to survive small sizes, thin strokes, glare, tired eyes and short exposure. A heading can look dominant because of its size and still be hard to read if its **value contrast** with the background is weak. Size gives prominence. Value contrast gives legibility. You need both.

The same applies in motion. A moving shape or caption is on screen for a limited time. If it blends into the background, the viewer loses it before they have understood it.

## {realWorld} Where this matters in practice
**Brand.** A logo is seen at the size of a browser tab, on a shop sign, on a coloured packaging panel, and over photography. A mark that relies on a faint tone difference will look unsure of itself, and may vanish on the one surface where it is needed most. Strong, simple value contrast is a large part of why confident identities read as high quality.

**Video and social.** Captions and titles are laid over footage you do not control. Bright skies, snow and busy textures routinely defeat white text. Editors add a soft darkening gradient, a solid bar, or a text shadow so the text works on every frame, not just the first.

**Motion graphics.** Animated explainers rely on the viewer tracking the right element. Designers keep the moving subject distinct from its background in value or colour, and quieten whatever is not meant to be followed.

## {goodVsBad} What separates a good fix from a bad one
| Problem | Weak fix | Strong fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grey text on cream is faint | Make it bold | Darken the colour until the ratio is comfortable |
| White caption lost on bright footage | Make the font thinner | Add a scrim or bar behind the text |
| Logo fades on a coloured panel | Make it larger | Use a version with stronger value difference for that surface |
| Moving shape gets lost | Slow it down | Separate it from the background in value or colour |

Weak fixes change something *other than the contrast* and so leave the problem in place. Bolder weight, size and speed can help, but they do not replace a real difference between the element and what is behind it.

When might you break this? A deliberate low-contrast texture behind a headline can add atmosphere, as long as the text that must be read keeps its contrast, and decoration never carries essential information.
