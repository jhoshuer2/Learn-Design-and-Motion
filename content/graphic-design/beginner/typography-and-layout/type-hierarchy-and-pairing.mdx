## {objective} What you will be able to do
By the end of this lesson you can:

- rank the text on a page into levels and give each level its own look;
- use a few sizes and weights, not many, and use them the same way every time;
- pair two typefaces so that one leads and one supports;
- spot pairings that clash or that are too alike.

## {concept} Levels, not decoration
Typography is how you make words easy to read and easy to rank. On any page some text matters most (a title), some explains (a description) and some is practical (a time or a place). **Type hierarchy** gives each kind its own level, so a viewer sees the order before reading a word.

You build levels with a few tools:

- **Size**: larger reads first.
- **Weight**: bolder reads as more important.
- **Value and colour**: darker or stronger text stands out from quieter text.
- **Case and spacing**: capitals with extra letter-spacing suit short labels, not paragraphs.

Use **three or four levels** at most on a simple page, and use each one the same way throughout. If every line has its own size, the hierarchy disappears.

**Pairing.** Most pages need at most **two typefaces**, with clear roles: one for headlines, one for body text. Two faces work together when they are **clearly different** (a serif with a sans serif, say) yet share a mood, or when you use one family in several weights. Two faces that are *almost* the same look like a mistake.

Two rules to carry through this module:

1. Fewer sizes, used consistently, beat many sizes used freely.
2. Roles first, then faces: decide what each level is for before choosing how it looks.

## {realWorld} Where you meet this every day
Look at a concert poster or a restaurant menu. The name of the event or restaurant is the largest text, dishes or acts are in a middle level, and prices or times are small and quiet. Section headings repeat the same style every time.

Now think of a notice board where every note uses a different font and size. Each note might be fine alone, but nothing tells you where to look first.

## {guided} Try it with support
Take a flyer or a menu you have nearby. Work through these steps:

1. Write down the text in the order you would want it read.
2. Group it into three or four levels and name each (for example title, item, detail).
3. Decide a size and weight for each level, and one typeface role for headings and one for body.
4. Check that no two levels look almost the same.

Keep your notes. You will use them in the independent task.
